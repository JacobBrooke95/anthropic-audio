A I for scientific discovery: How researchers use Claude.

In this report, we use privacy-preserving infrastructure to study the scientific use of Claude, Claude Code, and Claude Science. We document how usage varies across scientific fields and research tasks, identify what explains these usage patterns, and outline scenarios for how A I could accelerate research.

From Anthropic Research, published November 5, 2026. By Johannes Hermle, Ben Jones, Lukas Althoff, Ryan Heller, Thomas Lyttelton, Carolyn Stein, Nathan Wilmers, Maxim Massenkoff, and Peter McCrory.

Summary.

What we measure. A I could speed up scientific discovery, but by how much depends on what A I can do and how scientists use it. Using privacy-preserving methods, we study 207000 Claude and Claude Code research sessions to understand how Claude is used across scientific fields and research tasks. We are conducting this research to help the public better understand the state of A I for science today and assess the impact that A I may have on scientific discovery in the future. Where scientists use Claude. Relative to the number of researchers in each field, Claude is used more in the formal sciences, such as computer science, mathematics, and statistics, and less in health and lab-based physical sciences. Scientists primarily use Claude to analyze data and to write. Data analysis alone accounts for about one-third of sessions on Claude and nearly half of sessions on Claude Code. Scientists use it less for idea generation and data gathering, even though, in an accompanying survey, they rank those tasks as the most important for scientific discovery. That said, specialized A I tools designed for scientific work could narrow the gap between the research tasks scientists use A I for and the ones they consider most important. On Claude Science, for instance, idea generation makes up about twice the share of use that it does on Claude. How Claude use tracks A I capability. We create a measure of how well A I can, in theory, perform each research task. The tasks A I is best suited to (the top 20 percent on this measure) get two to four times as much Claude use as the time researchers spend on them would predict. The tasks A I is least suited to (the bottom 20 percent), which include most physical work, get very little. We observe the same pattern across fields: fields where A I can perform many tasks well, such as computer science and statistics, use Claude more. What this could mean for the speed of scientific discovery. Using a simple model, we explore scenarios for how much A I could speed up research. If existing A I capabilities are widely adopted, they could double research output in some fields. If A I could carry out all non-physical research work, output in fields such as computer science and statistics could rise as much as 15-fold, while output in bench sciences such as chemistry and biology, which are held back by physical bottlenecks, would rise three- or fourfold. In our survey of researchers who actively use A I, about 40 percent expect A I models to carry out full research projects on their own by 2030.

Introduction.

Science has been described as an “endless frontier” (Bush 1945). But how fast we explore that frontier has historically been limited by a scarce resource: the time and talent of human researchers. Artificial intelligence could ease that limit by amplifying what researchers can do, transforming both the process of discovery and the pace of exploration (Wang and others 2023; Amodei 2024). Few applications of A I could matter more for human welfare. Given the highly cognitive nature of research compared with most sectors of the economy, A I’s effects may be particularly large and early in this domain.

Recent examples show A I playing a growing role in scientific discovery. In structural biology, AlphaFold predicted the three-dimensional shapes of over 200 million proteins (Jumper and others 2021; Varadi and others 2024), and its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In materials science, GNoME predicted 380000 stable new crystals (Merchant and others 2023). In mathematics, researchers have used A I systems to help resolve decades-old Erdős problems, produce a potential proof for a Millennium Prize problem, and make progress on other major problems, from open conjectures to verifying landmark proofs (Anthropic 2026a, 2026b; OpenAI 2026a, 2026b; Tsoukalas and others 2026).

Beyond these developments, most scientific progress involves many specialized, interlinked research tasks, which can vary substantially across fields. How much A I accelerates scientific discovery depends not only on A I’s capabilities, but also on how it is adopted across these tasks. In this report, we use privacy-preserving infrastructure to study the scientific use of Claude, Claude Code, and Claude Science. We document how usage varies across fields and tasks, identify what explains these usage patterns, and outline scenarios for how A I could accelerate research.

Claude use in science varies by field. Relative to their number of researchers, formal sciences such as computer science, mathematics, and statistics use Claude more heavily, while health and lab-based physical sciences use it less. Across research tasks, scientists use Claude more for analyzing data and writing, and less for gathering data or generating ideas and hypotheses. This is consistent with self-reported A I use from an accompanying survey of scientists.

To understand these differences, we develop a measure of theoretical A I capability. Claude use is higher in tasks and fields that score higher on this measure. These also tend to be more cognitive in nature and better suited to reinforcement learning, in which A I learns from feedback on repeated attempts. In short, scientists appear to use A I where it works best.

Based on these findings, we outline scenarios for how A I could accelerate scientific progress. With current capabilities, research output in some fields could as much as double, primarily from task-level augmentation. Researchers in our survey expect much greater capabilities to come. In a hypothetical scenario where A I fully automates all non-physical work, highly cognitive fields such as computer science or statistics could see up to a 15-fold increase in output. Because the fields with the largest potential for acceleration include those that produce A I itself, these gains could create an additional feedback loop, in which faster A I research produces more capable A I, which in turn could further accelerate research.

While faster scientific discovery could bring substantial benefits to humanity, A I-driven research also carries risks and uncertainties. Less human oversight could lead to the pursuit of dangerous or unethical research. A I could also reshape researchers’ careers, making research work more or less rewarding, and alter the sense of purpose associated with the human pursuit of knowledge.

How we conducted this research.

Our goal with this research is to measure the different ways scientists use Claude while protecting the details of their work. To do this, we use privacy-preserving infrastructure to analyze the scientific use of Claude on consumer plans. A language model, used here as a “classifier,” reads each session transcript and labels it with a fixed set of categories, such as the field and the research task it involves, according to a predefined taxonomy (SciNet taxonomy by Alamos and Althoff 2026). The resulting labels, such as “chemistry” or “data cleaning,” are the only information from these conversations retained and analyzed by the research team for this report. No classifier employed in this study extracts and stores open-ended information or details on research content, such as research datasets, hypotheses, experimental methods, early findings, or unpublished papers. Results are reported only in aggregate and only for cells that meet a minimum number of distinct accounts. All classifiers introduced in this study are listed in the Appendix.

Our measurement involves two steps. A first classifier identifies whether a session transcript involves scientific work. A second classifier then assigns each of these transcripts to a field, subfield, and task in the SciNet taxonomy. SciNet contains 7262 tasks, spanning 34 fields and 318 subfields. Some tasks in the taxonomy, such as drafting manuscripts, are universal to all fields. Others are specific to individual fields or subfields. For instance, in the taxonomy, the task of predicting optical absorption and emission spectra is specific to materials science. Together, the tasks cover the full process of producing a new research finding.

Our main sample consists of 207049 sessions on consumer plans from August 2 to September 30, 2026, which were assigned a scientific field and a research task: 128914 from Chat and Cowork, two general-purpose interfaces for conversation and knowledge work, hereafter referred to as “Claude,” and 78135 from Claude Code, an agentic coding tool that works directly with a user’s files, writing and running code. We also study a supplemental sample of Claude Science sessions. For details on sample construction, see the Appendix.

Combining usage data and a structured taxonomy of research tasks allows us to study which fields adopt Claude and for which parts of the research process. This research contributes to a growing body of work attempting to measure the adoption of A I technology for scientific discovery (Kobak and others 2025; Liang and others 2025; Hao and others 2026; Codreanu and others 2026).

How Claude use varies across scientific fields.

We begin by describing how Claude use varies across scientific fields. Figure 1 compares a field’s share of sessions against the share of global researchers in that field, giving us a sense of relative use per scientific field. 1 On Claude, the three largest fields by session share are computer science (11.9 percent), engineering (8.4 percent), and medicine (7.7 percent), which are also among the largest fields by global researcher counts. 2 Overall, fields with a larger share of researchers also tend to account for a larger share of Claude sessions (log-log correlation of 0.79).

However, Claude use per field cannot be explained by the number of researchers alone. In Figure 1, fields above the 45-degree line show higher usage relative to their size, while fields below the line show lower usage. Relative usage is especially high for the formal sciences, including computer science, mathematics, and statistics. Usage is also high for the social sciences. By contrast, physical sciences such as chemistry, where work often happens in a lab, tend to show lower relative usage. So does medicine, the third-largest field by session volume, where researchers spend much of their time with patients.

On Claude Code, usage is less strongly related to field size (log-log correlation with researcher counts of 0.62) and concentrates in more quantitative fields. Despite making up only 4.3 percent of the world’s researchers, computer science alone accounts for 32.7 percent of Claude Code sessions. 3 Other formal sciences, such as mathematics and statistics, also show high relative usage. Relative usage is low in the arts and humanities and in the social sciences, with the exception of economics, a comparatively quantitative field.

How Claude use varies across research tasks.

Beyond understanding which fields use Claude, we also want to understand what scientists use it for. Research involves many different tasks, from generating hypotheses and analyzing data to writing up findings in a research paper. To better discern how Claude supports scientific discovery, we study how usage varies across these tasks. To do so, we assign each session to one of the 7262 tasks in SciNet. These tasks can be universal to all fields, such as drafting manuscripts, or specific to fields or subfields, such as performing differential gene expression analysis to compare tumor subtypes, which is associated with cancer biology. The taxonomy distinguishes between research tasks, which researchers perform to produce new scientific findings, and non-research tasks, such as teaching, peer review, and service to the department or field. 5 We limit our analysis to research tasks spanning eight categories: (1) data analysis, (2) writing and communication, (3) data gathering, (4) reading and knowledge acquisition, (5) theoretical analysis, (6) design and development (of software, materials, and creative works), (7) ideation and hypothesis generation, and (8) other, such as administering research.

For each task category, we calculate its share of Claude and Claude Code sessions separately. To assess whether usage in a category is high or low, we consider the share of their time that researchers typically spend on that category, as estimated by SciNet. These time estimates are based on an L L M classifier, validated using both wet-lab research protocols that record researcher time spent per task and the frequency of tasks across thousands of academic papers (see the Appendix ). This provides a benchmark to assess whether the share of Claude use across different task categories follows or deviates from how researchers spend their time.

Usage by task category. Data analysis tasks have the highest usage in absolute terms and are overrepresented relative to researcher time. Data analysis accounts for 33.7 percent of sessions on Claude and 48.1 percent on Claude Code, compared with 27.6 percent of researcher time. Writing and communication tasks also show high usage, especially on Claude (32.0 percent, compared with 16.0 percent of researcher time). 6 Data gathering tasks have low relative usage, accounting for 28.6 percent of researcher time but only 11.5 percent of sessions on Claude and 11.9 percent on Claude Code. The underrepresentation of data gathering is particularly strong for tasks with physical barriers, such as the collection of clinical or biochemical data. Data gathering tasks that can rely on more digital processes, such as retrieving archival documents or text, are less underrepresented, while purely digital data collection tasks, such as collecting online data, can be overrepresented. Ideation and hypothesis generation tasks also have low usage, accounting for 4.2 percent of sessions on Claude and 2.1 percent on Claude Code, compared with 7.1 percent of researcher time.

Comparing observed usage to survey evidence. Along with our analysis of Claude and Claude Code sessions, we conducted a survey of 528 scientists, whose self-reported A I usage lines up with the patterns we observed in those sessions. The sample consists of researchers primarily in the life and health sciences who are active adopters of A I. 7 Consistent with the Claude usage patterns shown in Figure 3, survey participants’ self-reported A I usage is highest for data analysis and writing and communication, and lower for ideation and hypothesis generation and data gathering. Participants also report more shared or delegated use of A I for data analysis and writing and communication (Figure 4).

The survey also asked which categories matter most for producing important research results. Respondents rank ideation and hypothesis generation first and data gathering second. Notably, these categories are underrepresented in self-reported usage as well as in Claude sessions. The reasons for this underrepresentation likely differ. Much of data gathering is physical, while ideation is not. For ideation, scientists may prefer generating ideas themselves, or they may perceive A I’s capabilities as limited. Our data may also undercount ideation, since it tends to be woven into many other research tasks and is difficult to classify. But A I may also be genuinely weaker at high-level ideation, such as choosing which research question to pursue and designing a study to answer it.

Biological and biomedical use of Claude Science. While Claude and Claude Code are general-purpose interfaces, specialized A I tools for the sciences are increasingly available. To study whether such tools support more specialized and demanding research, we use our privacy-preserving analysis infrastructure to compare biology and biomedical work on Claude Science with that on Claude and Claude Code.

Claude Science connects Claude to many research tools and databases that are otherwise fragmented and acts to integrate and accelerate scientific workflows. Scientists must often use different platforms, each with unique data structures or query languages, to combine distinct tools and databases in their research. For example, biologists studying whether existing drugs can act on a specific protein must typically consult separate databases regarding the protein’s function, its structure, compounds already tested against it, and related drug trials or papers. Claude Science combines these into one workflow.

Use of Claude Science is concentrated in the life sciences. The top two fields are biomedical sciences and biology, which together comprise over 50 percent of sessions. Within these fields, we assess which tasks are most overrepresented relative to tasks performed by Claude.

We find that researchers use Claude Science for more specialized tasks than Claude (Figure 5). For example, the task “Propose mechanistic models for protein to protein or protein to ligand interactions” is 5.6 x more likely to appear on Claude Science than on Claude. This task involves explaining, at the level of individual molecular contacts, how a protein binds to another protein or to a small molecule such as a drug. Claude Science is well positioned for this task, as it can gather protein structures and binding data from multiple connected databases and combine them into a testable model of the interaction. By contrast, Claude Science is used relatively less for general tasks such as drafting manuscripts or coordinating with collaborators. Moreover, we find that ideation and hypothesis generation accounts for 8 percent of Claude Science sessions, roughly on par with the share of researcher time these tasks take, suggesting that specialized A I tools for science can facilitate more complex and creative work.

Over 80 percent of Claude Science use is estimated to require doctoral-level training (20 years of education), compared with about 50 to 70 percent on other interfaces. Over 70 percent of Claude Science sessions are performed with a high degree of A I autonomy, far higher than on Claude (23.7 percent) and somewhat higher than on Claude Code (64.1 percent).

How use relates to capabilities.

Does A I usage track A I capabilities? Field size explains some of the variation in Claude use, but not all of it. For example, researchers in computer science use Claude far more than the field’s size predicts, while those in nutrition use Claude far less. Similarly, where researchers spend their own time does not fully predict where they use Claude the most. Some broad task categories, such as data analysis and writing, have a much larger share of usage than of research time, while others, such as data gathering and ideation and hypothesis generation, have a much smaller share. Here, we test whether these differences in usage reflect what A I can actually do.

To measure task-specific A I capabilities, we first construct capability scores using an L L M classifier. For each task, SciNet provides a list of individual steps that a scientist needs to perform to complete the task. For instance, in chemistry, one task involves purifying reaction mixtures, and one of that task’s steps involves packing a chromatography column. The classifier is shown pairs of steps across the taxonomy and asked which one A I can perform better. We aggregate these comparisons using a Bradley to Terry model, the same model behind the Elo ratings that rank chess players based on head-to-head results. A step’s score rises when it wins comparisons and falls when it loses them. We then average each task’s steps to obtain a task-level score (see the Appendix for details). We label this measure theoretical A I capability. We refer to a task’s specific score as its A I capability score.

To compare usage across tasks, we ask whether Claude use is higher or lower for a task, relative to the time researchers spend on that task. For example, a task that takes 5 percent of researchers’ time but accounts for 10 percent of Claude sessions would represent twice as much use as expected, a ratio of 2. A ratio of 1 indicates that Claude use is in line with researcher time spent on the task. A ratio below 1 indicates that Claude is used less than expected based on the amount of researcher time the task takes.

Figure 8 shows that this use ratio rises sharply with a task’s A I capability score. For tasks in the lowest 20 percent of capability scores, like purifying reaction mixtures and conducting participant observation, Claude is barely used. At median capability scores, the ratio is roughly 1, so usage is in line with researcher time. For tasks in the top 20 percent of capability scores, like visualizing data and applying statistical tests, usage is about twice as high on Claude and about four times as high on Claude Code, compared with the typical time researchers spend on those tasks. These patterns suggest that scientists’ use of Claude closely tracks A I capability scores. The steeper gradient for Claude Code reflects its focus on computational work, which has higher capability scores.

Theoretical A I capabilities can differ across tasks for many reasons. To understand these differences, we relate each task’s A I capability score to three groups of L L M-derived task characteristics: its suitability for reinforcement learning (R L); whether the work is predominantly cognitive, interpersonal, or physical; and whether regulatory restrictions limit A I use (Figure 9; see the Appendix for definitions and data sources).

Consider R L suitability. In R L, an A I model tries a task repeatedly and is rewarded when it succeeds, allowing the model to learn how to perform that task. Tasks are particularly suitable for R L when success is easy to verify, when many examples can be simulated in a computing environment, and when feedback can be delivered quickly (Lambert and others 2024; Guo and others 2025). We use task-specific measures from SciNet for these three R L-related components and summarize them in a single index. A task’s capability score is highly correlated with its R L index score, which was classified separately (correlation of 0.78). This suggests that A I is most capable at the research tasks where R L is easiest to apply.

Other important task characteristics concern the nature of the work. Some research tasks are fundamentally cognitive, while others require physical actions or interpersonal contact. Physical tasks in general are beyond the reach of purely cognitive machines. There can also be regulatory restrictions that limit A I use. A I capability scores are higher for tasks characterized by cognitive work (correlation of 0.69). By contrast, research tasks that involve more physical work have lower A I capability scores (-0.57). Scores also tend to be lower for research activities that include interpersonal work (-0.31) or that involve regulatory restrictions on A I use (-0.38).

The same task characteristics also predict how much researchers use Claude for a given task (Figure 10). Claude is barely used for tasks in the bottom fifth of R L suitability. For tasks in the top fifth, Claude use is disproportionately high relative to the time researchers spend on them: twice as high on Claude, and four times as high on Claude Code. This is similar to the association between Claude use and A I capability scores overall, where the top 20 percent of tasks also draw two to four times the expected use.

Claude use is also disproportionately high in predominantly cognitive tasks. By contrast, Claude use is low for physical and interpersonal tasks. Claude use is also lower for tasks where regulatory barriers limit A I assistance. Overall, across the spectrum of highly specialized research tasks, usage and capability measures are strongly related.

Different scientific fields rely on different mixes of research activities. Does the correlation between usage and capability scores for narrow research tasks help explain why some fields use Claude more than others? Our data shows it does: a subfield’s Claude use per researcher tends to rise with the average A I capability score across its tasks (correlations of 0.45 for Claude and 0.57 for Claude Code), as shown in Figure 11.

There is also substantial variation across domains, which suggests that Claude use is affected by factors beyond what A I can do. Health sciences researchers use Claude relatively little compared with the A I capability scores of their tasks, possibly because regulation of medical data and research limits the use of A I or makes it harder to integrate. Researchers in the formal sciences, social sciences, and the arts and humanities use Claude more than the A I capability scores would predict. Much of the work in these fields is done through text and code, which likely facilitates the adoption of A I tools. Claude use in the physical sciences and life sciences generally tracks A I capability scores more closely, though individual subfields can deviate.

In other words, not only do subfields with more theoretical A I capability use A I more, but they also use it in a more autonomous manner.

Research acceleration scenarios with A I.

The pace of scientific and technological progress depends on good ideas and the means to pursue them. Historically, both have been limited by human capacity. There are only so many researchers, and each has only so much time to formulate a hypothesis, design the right tests, run the analyses, and synthesize the findings.

A I could ease these constraints in two ways. First, it could take on work that researchers now do, allowing them to pursue more ideas. Second, it could contribute ideas that researchers might not otherwise identify. The second may ultimately matter most, but it is much harder to measure, so our analysis focuses on the first.

Approach.

We consider three scenarios of scientific acceleration through A I. Scenario 1 assumes wide adoption of current A I capabilities, as estimated from our survey and the A I capability scores. Scenarios 2 and 3 are hypothetical futures in which A I fully automates 75 percent or 100 percent of non-physical research steps, the individual actions that make up each research task in SciNet, starting with the steps that have the highest A I capability scores. In all three scenarios, researchers continue to perform physical steps. These are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts. They show how physical work, theoretical A I capability, and the cost of A I could shape the acceleration of scientific research, and which fields could accelerate the most.

For each scenario, we report two measures. The first is the A I share: the fraction of researcher time spent on a study that A I could take on. Given how researchers currently allocate their time, it measures how much human research time A I could free up, and it follows directly from our analyses above. Researchers can redirect that freed-up time to other work, increasing research output. Our second measure, the acceleration, estimates this gain: the increase in research output for a fixed research budget, relative to the research output without A I. It tells us how many more research results the same budget could produce. Freed-up time does not translate one-for-one into more output, both because researchers redirect their effort toward the tasks where A I helps less, and because A I itself has costs.

We build the A I share for Scenario 1 from our survey, which records five levels of A I use: none, assistive, shared, delegated, and autonomous. We assume these levels correspond to A I taking on 0 percent, 25 percent, 50 percent, 75 percent, and 100 percent of a researcher’s time for a given task step. Within each task category, we rank task steps by A I capability score. We then assign a level of A I use to each task step, in the same proportions reported in our survey, with the highest levels going to the highest-scoring steps. For ideation and hypothesis generation, for example, 17 percent of responses report no A I use, and 53 percent report assistive use. The 17 percent of ideation task steps with the lowest A I capability scores are therefore assigned no A I share, the next 53 percent are assigned 25 percent, and so on up the scale. Because respondents are likely to be heavy A I adopters, we treat their reported use as a proxy for what A I can do today. Finally, we aggregate researcher time freed up by A I from steps to fields (see the Appendix ).

To compute the acceleration, we use an ideas production function, a standard economic model of how research inputs, such as researcher time, are turned into research outputs. In this model, a paper requires many research tasks (Jones 2026). The tasks are complementary, such that strength in one cannot fully make up for weakness in another. As A I makes some tasks cheaper, researchers shift their effort toward tasks where A I helps less, thereby easing the bottlenecks that limit the overall gain. For example, researchers could spend more time coming up with new questions or running more physical experiments to expand empirical evidence. We calibrate how much these bottlenecks limit the overall gain using Ahmadpoor and Jones (2019). For A I’s cost, we assume that an hour of A I agent time costs 30, dollarscompared with 95 dollarsfor an hour of a researcher’s time, and that A I runs for 0.2 hours for each researcher hour it saves. These are illustrative assumptions: the A I cost uses today’s public list prices. The smaller these costs, the more research can be done within a given budget and the larger the acceleration, as we discuss below. The Appendix describes the model and assumptions.

Scenario 1: Current capabilities.

With current capabilities, if adopted widely, A I could take on 27 to 52 percent of the researcher time needed to produce a new study, with a median of 38 percent across fields (Figure 13, left panel). These shares imply research accelerations ranging from 1.35 x in the arts, 1.36 x in anthropology, and 1.41 x in chemistry to 1.96 x in computer science and 2.02 x in statistics (Figure 13, right panel). Accelerations remain limited because A I work today is mostly assistive or shared rather than autonomous, so researchers still spend substantial time on nearly every step. Even the most A I-ready fields, whose work is almost entirely cognitive, reach only about a 2 x acceleration. These estimates assume that A I diffuses widely and that researchers apply it to every step where it is useful today.

Two factors explain most of the differences across fields. The first is physical work, which A I cannot take on without physical integrations. In lab and field sciences such as the agricultural sciences, biomedical sciences, chemistry, and materials science, 38 to 41 percent of researcher time goes to physical steps, compared with 4 percent in the median field. The physical share alone explains about one-third of the variation in the A I share. The second factor is the distribution of A I capability scores within cognitive work. The arts and history spend little time on physical steps (10 percent and 4 percent, respectively), yet their cognitive steps have low A I capability scores, perhaps because qualitative and interpretive work is less amenable to reinforcement learning. Computer science and statistics, by contrast, have high scores and large A I shares, driven by data analysis, the type of work with the highest A I capability scores across fields.

Fields with a higher use ratio on Claude also tend to have larger A I shares (Figure 14). The A I share is calculated without any Claude use data, so this relationship is further evidence that A I use tracks theoretical A I capability.

Scenarios 2 and 3: Greater automation.

In these more speculative scenarios, which depend heavily on assumptions about A I’s reach and costs, greater automation raises both the A I share and the acceleration sharply, and widens the differences between fields.

In Scenario 2, where 75 percent of non-physical steps can be done by A I, accelerations reach about 8 x in computer science, 7 x in statistics and library and information science, and 6 x in economics. Most other social sciences, mathematics, and physics and astronomy reach 4 to 5 x, while bench and medical sciences reach about 2 to 3 x. The bench sciences and the arts and humanities gain the least, but for different reasons. In the bench sciences, much of the work is physical and must still be done by researchers. In the arts and humanities, the work is mostly cognitive but has low A I capability scores, so few of its steps are among those automated. Automated steps make up 82 percent of non-physical researcher time in computer science but only 28 percent in the arts. History, anthropology, and the arts accelerate by about 2 x or less.

In Scenario 3, the arts and humanities pull ahead, while the bench sciences remain held back. Once A I automates all non-physical steps, low A I capability scores no longer limit cognitive work, and A I can take on nearly all research work in many fields (Figure 13, left panel). In this speculative scenario, fields with few physical tasks, including statistics, computer science, economics, mathematics, psychology, and most of the social sciences and the arts and humanities, reach about 15 to 16 x. Fields with substantial lab or field work, such as biology, chemistry, materials science, agricultural sciences, and biomedical sciences, reach only 3 to 4 x. Absent automated labs, their physical steps, representing about 40 percent of researcher time, remain the bottleneck.

Two lessons follow from these scenarios. First, physical bottlenecks remain powerful. Steps that A I cannot perform without physical integrations come to dominate researcher time and budgets, capping acceleration. Second, where no such bottlenecks exist, extraordinary rates of progress become possible, and A I’s cost becomes the limit. Under our assumptions, a task that is fully automated by A I still costs about one-sixteenth of what it would cost a researcher to perform, so accelerations approach about 16 x and stop there. Lower A I costs would raise this ceiling proportionately, such that halving the cost of A I, for example, would double the rate of progress. If costs fall sharply, including for harder tasks that A I automates later, the fields without physical bottlenecks could accelerate far beyond these estimates.

These calculations have important limits that cut in both directions. We do not consider constraints at the level of a field rather than a research team. For instance, only so many experiments can run at facilities like the Large Hadron Collider. We also count only the cost of researcher and A I time, not the costs of machinery or inputs for experiments, which can be substantial in the health, physical, and life sciences. Nor do we consider simulations to replace physical experiments, which could ease bottlenecks in some fields. Finally, we do not consider the quality of ideas. If A I identifies better hypotheses with higher chances of success, research could accelerate further; if research taste remains uniquely human, the acceleration would be slower.

Researcher expectations.

The scenarios above characterize a range of possibilities. Researchers’ own expectations can provide a unique guide grounded in expertise. To gather their viewpoints, our survey of researchers in the life and health sciences asked when they thought A I, working on its own, would match the best researchers in their field for different categories of research work (Figure 15). Respondents’ expectations are high in the near term: over half expect A I to match top researchers at data analysis by 2027, and 38 percent say that A I has already reached this level. By 2030, 94 percent expect A I will match the best researchers in data analysis, 89 percent in writing, and 72 percent each in ideation and data gathering. Expectations for ideation and data gathering are lower, consistent with the lower use of Claude for these tasks today.

Expectations for end-to-end A I research without humans in the loop are more conservative, but researchers still expect remarkable progress. About 40 percent of respondents expect A I to carry out full research projects by 2030, and about 64 percent by 2035, although 16 percent say it will never happen. Expectations that A I will autonomously produce scientific breakthroughs are more cautious still, with approximately half of researchers expecting this to occur only after 2035, if ever. Overall, many researchers expect high levels of automation in the next decade, broadly in line with our greater automation scenarios. How fast and to what extent these expectations materialize will depend not only on advances in A I as a software technology, but also on efforts to build automated labs that overcome physical barriers.

Comparing past survey evidence to realized outcomes suggests that A I progress has often been dramatically underestimated (Rosenberg and others 2026). In a survey in mid-2022, for example, the median estimate for A I achieving gold-level performance in the International Mathematical Olympiad was 2030 among domain experts and 2035 among superforecasters (Karger and others 2023). A I achieved this milestone in 2025, some five to 10 years ahead of those predictions. While forecasters have systematically underestimated progress in A I capabilities, there are a few exceptions, primarily in domains that require execution in the physical world, such as A I-assisted laboratory tasks in biology.

Discussion.

If A I helps speed up scientific discovery, it could contribute to higher standards of living and faster progress in treating disease. Our estimates suggest the potential gains are substantial. But advancements in A I’s research capabilities do not automatically translate into rapid progress; achieving such acceleration will also depend on how we respond to several constraints.

First, adoption can substantially lag capabilities. Diffusion of new technologies is typically slow (Comin and Hobijn 2010), and productivity gains tend to materialize only after organizations restructure around them (David 1990; Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson 2021). Individual learning processes, friction at the organizational or system level, and constraints on A I access or infrastructure can all slow adoption.

Second, research may continue to face bottlenecks that A I does not remove. Physical experimentation, which can be costly and slow, can substantially limit progress even with extremely capable A I. We see this especially in the bench sciences in the scenarios above. Choosing what to study may be another bottleneck. Research questions largely determine a research project’s value, and A I’s capacity to generate good questions remains unclear. If ideation is a bottleneck, it will limit acceleration even as answering a given question becomes much cheaper. Conversely, if A I outstrips humans in formulating high-quality hypotheses, acceleration could be more substantial, especially in domains where testing hypotheses is costly.

Third, A I-enabled research could raise the quantity or quality of research output, with different implications for science. A surge in new research papers could advance a field faster but also overwhelm scientists’ capacity to absorb or verify the results. On the other hand, new technologies, from microscopes and telescopes to statistical software, have historically raised the quality of scientific insights, and A I can likewise make more comprehensive analyses and more ambitious projects feasible. And as papers become easier to produce, the research community may increasingly reward work that is especially novel or high quality. Whether more efficient research leads to greater quantity or greater quality will depend on researchers’ choices and the incentives set by journals, funders, and institutions.

Fourth, the way research is organized may change in ways that are hard to anticipate. As A I becomes more capable and autonomous, A I systems could take the lead in verification, refereeing, and communicating important results, which would ease the absorption problem discussed above. Further in the future, A I research agents might also push the frontiers of science in large communities of their own, independently of humans, using robotics and automated labs. Such autonomous A I research would raise questions about how humans keep track of new knowledge, whether they can understand it, and how it is generated.

Fifth, more autonomous A I systems may increase the challenge of ensuring that A I-driven research remains aligned with human goals. Keeping humans involved in key research decisions, such as which questions to pursue and how to pursue them, may matter for safety and human wellbeing as A I agents become more powerful and creative. Discoveries themselves, or the byproducts of research, can also carry risks, particularly in biology, where the same capabilities that speed up medical advances could also produce dangerous knowledge or materials. In such cases, deliberate checkpoints in the research process may be warranted, requiring us to accept slower progress to reduce risk.

Finally, the work that researchers undertake may change substantially. One effect may be a change in researchers’ job satisfaction. If A I takes over enjoyable or rewarding tasks, satisfaction may fall and fewer people may choose research careers. If A I instead takes over tasks that researchers find tedious, satisfaction may rise. Administrative work is one potential target. In the U S, principal investigators on federally funded projects report spending about 44 percent of their research time meeting administrative requirements rather than doing research (Schneider and others 2020), and easing this burden could be a large boon, saving time and improving overall satisfaction. Ultimately, the net effect is uncertain and may change as A I’s capabilities evolve. An optimistic view is that A I will expand opportunities for discovery, which is core to what makes research careers rewarding. If science is a voyage into the “endless frontier,” A I may be a new vessel, allowing researchers to travel farther across a vast expanse of research questions and discoveries.
